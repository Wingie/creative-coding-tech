Echo Garden

A gridless instrument for live sets, where the music moves when bodies and plants move.

Picture a potted plant on a wooden table. A small dark box sits beside it, with one cable clipped to a leaf and one pushed into the soil. A desktop robot arm reaches over and rests its tool on a leaf. The box lights up, notes rise from the plant, and slow rings spread from the pot. The arm lifts, the notes stop, and the rings fade.

A robot arm rests its tip on a leaf, and the plant joins the band. A little box called a Pocket Scion listens to the plant's electrical signal and turns it into MIDI notes, which play instruments in a live Ableton set. When the arm lifts, the plant goes quiet, but what it already played keeps going round in the loop.

How it works

  1. The plant

    Two clips wire the plant to the Scion, one on a leaf and one in the soil. The Scion only plays on its own pulse, a little under eight times a second. A threshold decides how many of those notes get through, and the sting and a few other instruments in the set follow the same threshold.

  2. The arm

    Touching a leaf closes the plant's circuit and lets its notes in. Lifting off stops them. Each spot the arm can reach plays a different set of notes, and in every new room the arm has to learn where the leaves are before the show.

  3. The body

    A camera watches the performer's hands, and a Kinect reads the whole body. Wekinator learns how each performer moves, so the same gesture means the same thing for whoever is playing. Bringing the arm to the performer at the nearest leaf, and lighting the body with the projector, are still being tried.

  4. The loop

    Frippertronics is looping over yourself through a long tape delay, the way Robert Fripp and Brian Eno did it with two tape machines. Here the plant's notes land on three rings that loop every 3.1, 5.3 and 8.6 seconds. Those lengths don't divide into each other, so the rings drift apart and never line up again. A note gets a little quieter each lap until it's gone, and new notes pile on top. The performer can sing into the loop too, or play it by hand like a theremin.

What the performer plays

  1. A theremin in the air

    The right hand sets the pitch and the left hand shapes the tone. There are four ways to play it: one note at a time, sliding between notes, a held drone, or a repeating arpeggio. Whatever the hands do, the notes stay in the key of the set, so nothing lands wrong.

  2. Hand signs pick the voice

    A victory sign on one hand and a finger count on the other switch between the drums, the bass and the lead. On the bass or the drums the theremin falls silent and the hands take over the sound instead: how busy the line is, its tone, and how much of it falls into the dub delays. A card along the bottom of the performer's screen shows every sign, and a ring round the hand fills up while a sign is held, so it's clear when it has been read.

  3. Sequencing through tape delay

    There is no grid. The rhythm comes from delay, the way Robert Fripp built whole pieces from two tape machines: a phrase goes into a long delay, comes back a little quieter on every pass, and new phrases are laid on top. Several delays and loops run at once, each with its own length, and the lead is sent into one of them at a time. Where it is sent decides what repeats, and riding the feedback decides how long it is remembered. A voice or a guest's instrument can be sent into the loop the same way.

  4. Asking for sounds out loud

    A small mic replaces the foot pedals. The performer says something like "make a sample of dusty dub chords", and a few seconds later a new loop exists, in the tempo and key of the set, waiting in its own scene. Two thumbs up mixes it in over eight bars, easing the tempo across. The performer's screen shows what was heard and what happened next.

Where it's at

The performer talks into a clip-on mic, with no button to press. An AI agent listens to everything she says and works out what she meant, even when a word is misheard or a request comes out in pieces. It ignores chatter and acts on real requests.

Each change waits in a queue. Her screen shows what is playing now, what comes next and a countdown to it. The music changes one thing at a time, at most once a minute. She can veto any move, and the hardware mixer always has the last word.

The set runs on four decks, one for each layer of the music: a bed, a rhythm, a melody and a texture. A scene is the set of layers that are on. Ask for a sound out loud and it is made in the set's tempo and key, then comes in on the bar.

Left to itself, the agent improvises. Once a minute it makes one move. It might swap a layer, bring one in or take one out, or hand the bass or the lead to another instrument. Whatever it hears, even nonsense, it turns into something musical.

It also takes a direction. Saying "slowly move the set to Indian folk music" walks the set there over about ten minutes, one layer at a time: a tanpura drone first, then tabla, bansuri and harmonium. The key can stay, because the set's C Dorian is already the raga Kafi. From there the set can travel on to Japan, with koto, taiko, shakuhachi and a temple bell.

The visuals follow her hands, the theremin and the plant. A higher note lifts the image, a louder one brightens it, and each note sends out a burst. The plant keeps reactions of its own. She can change the picture by voice too, with something like "show the cathedral".

The hand theremin has four modes, picked with hand signs, and a short training round learns each performer's hands. The robot arm joins the plant on the last day of the hackathon.
